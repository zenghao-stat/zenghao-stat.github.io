PAC Reasoning: Controlling the Performance Loss for Efficient Reasoning

TL;DR

  1. PAC Reasoning provides statistical control for selective thinking, which routes between a non-thinking model and a thinking model. It calibrates the switching threshold to a user-specified tolerance for loss relative to the thinking model.
  2. Test a fixed grid of thresholds in increasing order and stop at the first failed upper-bound test. This rule does not require the estimated bounds to be monotone.
  3. On MATH-500, the logits-based version saves 37.61% of tokens on average at a binary-loss tolerance of \(\epsilon=0.03\). The paper reports both means and run-level results; the charts below let you inspect the trade-off.

Abstract

Large reasoning models (LRMs) have achieved remarkable progress in complex problem-solving tasks. Despite this success, LRMs typically suffer from high computational costs during deployment, highlighting a need for efficient inference. A practical direction is selective thinking, which switches the LRM between thinking and non-thinking modes dynamically. However, such approaches often introduce additional reasoning errors and lack statistical guarantees for the performance loss, which are critical for high-stakes applications. In this work, we propose Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) Reasoning that controls the performance loss under the user-specified tolerance. Specifically, we construct an upper confidence bound on the performance loss and determine a threshold for switching to the non-thinking model. Theoretically, using the threshold to switch between the thinking and non-thinking modes ensures bounded performance loss in a distribution-free manner. Our experiments on reasoning benchmarks show that the proposed method can save computational budgets and control the user-specified performance loss.

01 / The idea

Why calibrate selective thinking?

Reasoning models can spend many tokens on easy questions. Switching to a non-thinking model saves work, but may add errors. PAC Reasoning calibrates selective thinking, with an explicit tolerance for the loss relative to a reference thinking model.

First, generate a fast answer and measure its uncertainty. Accept it when the score is below a calibrated threshold; otherwise, call the thinking model. A statistical upper bound, rather than the score alone, sets the threshold.

02 / The method

Calibrating selective thinking

A fixed sequence of tests turns a pointwise risk bound into a guarantee for the selected policy.

Non-thinking modelAnswer + uncertainty \(U(x)\)
→
Calibrated threshold \(\hat u\)Accept if \(U(x)<\hat u\)
→
Final answerOtherwise, use the thinking model
A

Score the fast answer

Use a logits-based or verbalized uncertainty score. The score guides routing; its quality affects the savings.

B

Calibrate in order

Test a pre-specified grid of thresholds from small to large. Stop at the first bound above the tolerance.

C

Route new prompts

Use the preceding threshold. Low uncertainty keeps the fast answer; high uncertainty routes to the thinking model.

03 / The guarantee

Performance-loss control

\[\mathbb{P}\!\left(R\!\left(\hat f_{\hat u}\right)\le\epsilon\right)\ge 1-\alpha.\]

Confidence over calibration data and UCB randomization.

The risk is the expected nonnegative loss relative to the thinking model. For binary loss, it counts cases where the thinking model is correct and the routed answer is wrong. It is not the model’s total error rate.

The selection theorem uses a fixed finite grid, non-decreasing true risk, valid pointwise population-risk upper bounds, and stopping at the first failed test. It does not require the estimated upper bounds to be monotone.

What the guarantee depends on

The model pair and score are fixed before calibration, and calibration and deployment follow the stated sampling setup. A bound for a fixed empirical pool alone does not establish population-risk coverage.

The CLT construction gives asymptotic validity under its regularity conditions. The Hoeffding construction gives finite-sample validity under its stated boundedness and sampling-weight conditions. The selection rule inherits the validity of the bound used.

A held-out batch can fluctuate above its population risk. The paper gives a separate bound for empirical test loss, with an extra test-sample tail term. Distribution shift requires fresh justification or recalibration.
